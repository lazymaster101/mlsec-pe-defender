# Literature review B: deployment implications

Reviewed the supplied PDFs using the page-separated full-text extractions in `tmp/papers`. Page numbers below are PDF page numbers (not printed proceedings pagination). Results describe each paper's own evaluation, never measurements of this repository's defender. The challenge requires a **binary Windows PE detector**, FPR ≤1%, TPR ≥95%, ≤1 GB RAM, and ≤5 s end-to-end latency; multiclass accuracy, F-measure, average runtime, and filtered-set accuracy cannot establish those requirements.

## 3605764.3623919 — Lookin' Out My Backdoor! Investigating Backdooring Attacks Against DL-driven Malware Detectors

Source: [local PDF](/Users/seshadithyasaravanan/Desktop/CSCE%20704/Model/704papers/3605764.3623919.pdf), 12 pages; substantive study pp. 1–10, references pp. 10–12.

- **Method and evidence:** MalConv/AvastNet dataset-poisoning attacks target model updates. The threat models distinguish clean/dirty labels, architecture/weight/data knowledge, and static byte versus latent-space triggers (pp. 3–6). Models initially use 250k malicious plus 250k benign samples; malware is temporally separated, but benign samples are not, and the study uses balanced classes (p. 6). Table 1 (p. 7) reports 100% triggered-sample evasion for a clean-label latent MalConv backdoor at 0.1% poisoning with full model knowledge; weaker-knowledge settings report 32% and 38% evasion at different poisoning budgets. These are attack outcomes, not intrinsic clean classifier TPRs or challenge measurements.
- **Limits and defenses:** Simple static triggers were removable through clean fine-tuning; that does not establish a remedy for latent triggers. STRIP's distributions overlap and MNTD performs near chance for the latent attack (pp. 8–10). Modifications to those defenses are explicitly future work. The paper cautions that erasing all non-executable regions can remove evidence from packed/obfuscated payloads; a dataset dominated by unpacked code cannot validate this sanitizer generally (pp. 3–4).
- **Design consequence:** Package immutable, provenance-recorded model weights with hashes. Do not update models from attacker-controlled requests or self-generated labels. Treat original/sanitized views as separately validated features or models, retaining the original view. Do not advertise a generic backdoor detector or static-trigger sanitization as proven protection. STRIP's 100 mixed evaluations per sample and MNTD's hundreds of shadow models are inappropriate additions to a constrained inference path without clear benefit and measurement.

## DeepSign — Deep Learning for Automatic Malware Signature Generation and Classification

Source: [local PDF](/Users/seshadithyasaravanan/Desktop/CSCE%20704/Model/704papers/DeepSign_Deep_learning_for_automatic_malware_signature_generation_and_classification.pdf), 8 pages; method pp. 3–4, evaluation pp. 5–7, references p. 8.

- **Method and evidence:** Runs each executable in Cuckoo, converts sandbox-report unigram presence into a 20,000-dimensional bit vector, and learns an eight-layer denoising-autoencoder representation ending in a 30-value signature (pp. 3–4, 6). The study has 1,800 malicious samples, 300 per each of six malware families; 1,200 train and 600 test. Ground truth comes from Kaspersky, including ambiguous families sharing code. The reported 98.6% is six-way malware-family classification accuracy after supervised fine-tuning; signature SVM and 1-NN achieve 96.4% and 95.3% in that same task (pp. 5–7).
- **Applicability:** There is no benign class and therefore no benign-file FPR evidence. Sandbox execution and a first encoder layer with over 100 million parameters do not fit a straightforward small, self-contained static service; the paper does not establish a ≤5 s execution budget or ≤1 GB runtime (p. 6).
- **Design consequence:** Do not port this pipeline for the challenge. Its transferable idea is learned compact representations, but the supplied static features/weights must be evaluated independently. Never cite 98.6% as this defender's expected TPR.

## DroidEvolver — Self-Evolving Android Malware Detection System

Source: [local PDF](/Users/seshadithyasaravanan/Desktop/CSCE%20704/Model/704papers/DroidEvolver_Self-Evolving_Android_Malware_Detection_System.pdf), 16 pages; architecture/algorithms pp. 3–6, experiments pp. 7–14, limitations pp. 14–15.

- **Method and evidence:** Android API-call presence feeds five online linear learners (PA, OGD, AROW, RDA, Ada-FOBOS). A buffered score-distribution comparison identifies drift; non-aging models vote and supply pseudo-labels to update aging models and expand the feature vocabulary (pp. 3–6). Data comprises 33,294 benign and 34,722 malicious APKs from 2011–2016, with roughly balanced class counts. The detailed 2012 test reports TPR 93.39% and FPR 1.70%, already outside the challenge targets even in its own domain (pp. 7, 11–12). The 1.37 s average runtime includes APK preprocessing on a machine with 12 GB RAM; it is neither a worst-case bound nor a 1 GB result (pp. 12–13).
- **Limits:** Android APIs and APK decompilation cannot be directly applied to Windows PE. API-only visibility misses dynamically loaded code; poisoning susceptibility and erroneous pseudo-label updates are acknowledged. Its obfuscation experiment tests 11 ordinary transformations on 100 malware samples; it does not establish resilience against adaptive attacks (pp. 13–14).
- **Design consequence:** Transfer temporal holdouts, explicit drift monitoring, and cheap-model comparisons to offline evaluation. Keep competition inference immutable and deterministic; do not adopt request-driven pseudo-label retraining. A growing vocabulary plus retained buffers requires explicit memory accounting, even when per-model classification is cheap.

## Functionality-Preserving Black-Box Optimization of Adversarial Windows Malware (GAMMA)

Source: [local PDF](/Users/seshadithyasaravanan/Desktop/CSCE%20704/Model/704papers/Functionality-Preserving_Black-Box_Optimization_of_Adversarial_Windows_Malware.pdf), 10 pages; methods pp. 2–5, experiments pp. 5–8, defense limitations p. 9. The image-only tables on p. 7 were visually checked.

- **Method and evidence:** Studies functionality-preserving benign-content padding and new-section injection against MalConv and a 2,381-feature EMBER GBDT. The optimization trades detector output against added bytes; structural and behavioral manipulation classes are distinguished (pp. 3–5). On 15k benign and 15k malicious files outside the original training evaluation, the published default GBDT threshold 0.8336 achieves **3.9% FPR / 95% TPR**, and MalConv threshold 0.5 achieves **3.5% FPR / 69% TPR** (p. 6). This directly demonstrates that a supplied threshold is not a portable FPR guarantee.
- **Attack/evaluation implications:** The study uses 500 malware samples for attack evaluation; optimized benign content is more effective than random bytes, and attacks still work with labels only (pp. 6–7). Table I reports detection rates as low as 14% for GBDT and 4% for MalConv under one section-injection setting, but those numbers belong to that experiment. Table II reports mean query costs near 0.60 s for GBDT versus 0.86–0.93 s for MalConv on its workstation, not a challenge-container worst-case guarantee. UPX raises benign-file malicious scores, illustrating a packing/label shortcut (p. 7).
- **Design consequence:** Prefer a compact feature model as the deployment baseline, but benchmark extraction plus inference and recalibrate with representative benign files. Include benign-content padding, section changes, and packed benign software in held-out robustness/error analysis. A label-only interface reduces information disclosure but is not a robustness defense. This paper does not validate its proposed countermeasures; robust features, adversarial retraining and query-sequence monitoring remain suggestions (p. 9).

## Mal-LSGAN: An Effective Adversarial Malware Example Generation Model

Source: [local PDF](/Users/seshadithyasaravanan/Desktop/CSCE%20704/Model/704papers/Mal-LSGAN_An_Effective_Adversarial_Malware_Example_Generation_Model.pdf), 6 pages; model pp. 2–3, results/ablations pp. 4–6.

- **Method and evidence:** A least-squares GAN modifies 128-dimensional API-presence vectors; the generator combines malware features with 20 noise dimensions and retains original features using a maximum operation (pp. 2–3). Data includes 2,733 malware and 1,357 benign samples, split 40% attack training, 40% detector training and 20% testing. The paper reports malware from VirusShare, benign samples from AndroZoo, and Cuckoo API extraction, a provenance/domain combination requiring caution before interpreting the baseline. It does not demonstrate reconstruction and semantic validation of modified executable files.
- **Results and limits:** Its tables show reduced detector TPR on synthetic feature vectors, varying substantially by target and transfer setting. The often quoted MLP 97.81%→2.92% concerns that attack experiment, not PE deployment accuracy. The activation/loss ablation does not uniformly favor least-squares loss: CE2 has lower MLP TPR (p. 5). Baseline FPRs for most models exceed 1% (p. 4). Lower transfer success against RF does not prove tree-model robustness to realizable PE transformations.
- **Design consequence:** Use the paper to motivate independent, diverse adaptive robustness tests, not to select an unvalidated GAN defense. Exclude GAN training and sandbox execution from inference. Any future adversarial training must use realizable, functionality-checked samples or clearly label feature-space-only results.

## Online Binary Models are Promising for Distinguishing Temporally Consistent Computer Usage Profiles

Source: [local PDF](/Users/seshadithyasaravanan/Desktop/CSCE%20704/Model/704papers/Online_Binary_Models_are_Promising_for_Distinguishing_Temporally_Consistent_Computer_Usage_Profiles.pdf), 12 pages; user study pp. 3–4, methods pp. 5–7, results pp. 7–9, limitations pp. 10–11. Referenced supplemental appendices are not contained in this 12-page supplied PDF.

- **Method and evidence:** An eight-week Windows 10 user study retains 31 of 63 enrolled participants, recording process, network, mouse and keyboard-activity metadata. TF-IDF process/domain features and activity counts feed per-user binary or one-class classifiers using 1–60 minute windows. Training uses the first seven days; online evaluation uses test-then-train with subsequent samples (pp. 3–7). Binary models outperform tested one-class alternatives; the headline 99.90% maximum F-score is identity/profile recognition, not malware detection (pp. 8–10).
- **Applicability limits:** No executable classification or malware sample evaluation occurs; the paper assumes users' systems are uncompromised. Most subjects are young university adults, and pandemic-period behavior constrains generalization. Its useful time window is around ten minutes, incompatible with classifying an isolated PE within five seconds (pp. 10–11).
- **Design consequence:** Out of scope for the HTTP PE classifier. Retain only the evaluation lesson that chronological splits and correctly ordered online evaluation matter. Do not add user/network telemetry collection or online profile learning to this product.
