# Paper review A: the first 13 PDFs in filename order

This review uses the supplied PDFs and their page-indexed text in `tmp/papers`. It examines methods, evaluations, limitations, and appendices beyond the abstracts. Page references below mean the PDF page index, starting at 1; printed proceedings page numbers sometimes differ. Graphs were not remeasured, and results from other datasets, years, tasks, or hardware are not treated as measurements of the delivered detector.

The relevant target is a static Windows PE detector packaged behind HTTP, with FPR ≤ 1%, TPR ≥ 95%, RAM ≤ 1 GB, and response time ≤ 5 seconds for each sample. Five papers have direct methodological relevance; the others inform engineering practice or explain why a proposed component is outside scope.

## 1. Passphrase and keystroke dynamics authentication: Usable security

**Source:** [1-s2.0-S0167404820302017-main.pdf](</Users/seshadithyasaravanan/Desktop/CSCE 704/Model/704papers/1-s2.0-S0167404820302017-main.pdf>), 13 pages; Bhana and Flowerday.

**Evidence.** The design-science study combines a login experiment with expert review. It enrolled 123 people, with 112 completing the login experiment, and asked them to create a conventional password and a lowercase passphrase of at least 16 characters, then log in repeatedly. Purposeful sampling limits population generalization (p. 2). Ten experts assessed security and usability (p. 3). The middle of the paper develops a two-tier authentication design, keystroke-dynamics options, theoretical entropy calculations, and an ordinal comparison of authentication methods (pp. 4–9). The login analysis reports fewer passphrase failures, and the expert review recommends gradual calibration of behavioral monitoring (pp. 9–12).

**Applicability and decision.** This is user authentication, not malware detection. Its entropy calculations concern human secrets, not PE byte entropy. Neither the login success rate nor its false-positive discussion measures binary-classification FPR. It supplies no PE model, dataset, or resource benchmark. Do not add keystroke dynamics, authentication scoring, or a password-entropy rule to the detector. The general lesson that false positives affect usability is already captured more directly by the challenge's explicit FPR constraint.

## 2. Machine Unlearning

**Source:** [1912.03817v3.pdf](</Users/seshadithyasaravanan/Desktop/CSCE 704/Model/704papers/1912.03817v3.pdf>), 19 pages; Bourtoule et al.

**Evidence.** SISA means Sharded, Isolated, Sliced, and Aggregated training. Samples influence one isolated constituent model; checkpoints between slices allow retraining to resume before a deleted sample entered training. Inference aggregates the constituent models (pp. 5–7). The objective is distributional unlearning relative to training without the removed records, not a malware classifier or a poisoning-robustness guarantee (pp. 1–5). Analytical and experimental sections quantify retraining savings and the accuracy cost of reducing examples per shard; complex tasks lose more accuracy with aggressive sharding (pp. 8–12). Evaluations use MNIST, Purchase, SVHN, CIFAR-100, ImageNet, and mini-ImageNet; the experimental GPU memory sizes are 12/16 GB, so they do not demonstrate the requested deployment budget (pp. 9–10). Distribution-aware sharding changes the accuracy/retraining tradeoff (pp. 13–14); appendices give cost derivations and aggregation/sample-count experiments (pp. 16–19).

**Applicability and decision.** No direct evidence supports SISA as a PE evasion defense. Multiple resident constituent models could make the 1 GB limit harder to satisfy. Retain reproducible training manifests, input hashes, labels, and model provenance so a later dataset correction can trigger retraining. Do not add SISA to the HTTP inference path; consider it only if efficient deletion becomes a separate training requirement.

## 3. Automatic Yara Rule Generation Using Biclustering

**Source:** [2009.03779v1.pdf](</Users/seshadithyasaravanan/Desktop/CSCE 704/Model/704papers/2009.03779v1.pdf>), 12 pages; Raff et al.

**Evidence.** AutoYara learns family-specific signatures from a few related files. It extracts long byte n-grams, filters globally common and low-information patterns, and uses adaptive biclustering to combine related patterns into logical clauses (pp. 3–5). The 600,000-file EMBER 2017 training corpus supplies background Bloom filters; these are about 33 MB per n-gram size on disk. Rule construction is an offline operation targeting minutes, not a five-second request operation (pp. 4–5). The paper evaluates 184 families, a held-out 400,000-file corpus, retro-hunts, and work on 24 production families (pp. 5–8). For 21/24 production families it produced useful low-FP rules, but it did not produce adequate signatures for every family. The retro-hunt table includes substantial benign contamination for some rules and estimates label proportions from at most 50 returned hits; those hit proportions are not the population FPR (p. 7). Structural inconsistency, noisy labels, and packing limit effectiveness (pp. 2–3, 9–10). Appendices explain clustering fallbacks and the more expensive VxSig comparison (pp. 11–12).

**Applicability and decision.** A compact, precompiled, locally validated signature layer could supplement a statistical PE detector. It is not sufficient evidence for 95% malware recall, especially for unseen families or encoded payloads. Train/select signatures offline; measure the aggregate rule set on disjoint benign data, because individually low-FP rules can collectively exceed the FPR budget. Cap scan time and rule count. Reject generic signatures for common libraries, packers, installers, or resource structures unless independent validation demonstrates their specificity. Do not run AutoYara training or family clustering per HTTP request.

## 4. Machine Learning (In) Security: A Stream of Problems

**Source:** [2010.16045v2.pdf](</Users/seshadithyasaravanan/Desktop/CSCE 704/Model/704papers/2010.16045v2.pdf>), 34 pages; Ceschin et al.

**Evidence.** This paper treats the complete security-ML pipeline: collection, attribute extraction, feature construction, model training/updating, and evaluation (pp. 4–6). It warns that random time mixing, uncertain labels, source assumptions, temporal resampling, unrealistic family selection, and regional dataset differences can inflate results (pp. 6–11). Feature extraction may itself drift; updating a classifier while freezing a vocabulary does not necessarily repair the representation (pp. 13–15). The discussion separates feature robustness from clean accuracy and uses benign-content dilution and droppers as examples (pp. 14–16). It covers drift, adversarial training, imbalance, transfer learning, and implementation constraints (pp. 16–22). Accuracy alone is misleading on imbalanced problems; evaluation must match the intended deployment, including label delay and processing limits (pp. 22–25). Its AndroZoo example shows that adding drift detection can be worse when labels arrive late, so online adaptation is not automatically beneficial (p. 24). The final discussion rejects blanket claims that ML recognizes all genuinely new threats (pp. 25–27); remaining pages are references.

**Applicability and decision.** This is the strongest evaluation guide in this subset. Keep training, calibration, and final test sets distinct; deduplicate across them and group known variants together. Prefer chronological evaluation when timestamps are reliable. Fit encoders, feature selection, and thresholds on training/calibration data only. Report confusion counts, FPR, TPR, timeouts, and confidence intervals on representative held-out PE files, with family/time/source slices where possible. Treat feature extraction time and memory as part of the detector. Keep production inference immutable rather than self-training on its own labels. Historical reported extraction timings are illustrative, not a substitute for measuring this image.

## 5. Asleep at the Keyboard? Assessing the Security of GitHub Copilot's Code Contributions

**Source:** [2108.09293v3.pdf](</Users/seshadithyasaravanan/Desktop/CSCE 704/Model/704papers/2108.09293v3.pdf>), 15 pages; Pearce et al.

**Evidence.** The study creates security-relevant generation scenarios, discards or minimally repairs non-compiling outputs, and evaluates the targeted weakness with CodeQL or manual review (pp. 3–5). For the software scenarios it reports 477 vulnerable programs out of 1,084 valid outputs and vulnerable top suggestions in 24/54 scenarios (pp. 5–6). Subsequent sections show prompt sensitivity and hardware-security examples (pp. 9–13). The authors discuss CodeQL coverage, narrow scenario design, manual collection, and changing remote model behavior as validity limits; a test for one CWE is not proof that a program lacks other flaws (pp. 13–14).

**Applicability and decision.** It supports review and testing of generated service/parser code. It is not evidence about detecting malware and supplies no reason to use an LLM for PE inference. Inspect input-size limits, integer/bounds handling, subprocess use, malformed-file behavior, and container privileges. Test these concrete boundaries rather than relying on a model's confidence or successful compilation. Its historical Copilot vulnerability percentages must not be attributed to current models or to this implementation.

## 6. Examining Zero-Shot Vulnerability Repair with Large Language Models

**Source:** [2112.02125v3.pdf](</Users/seshadithyasaravanan/Desktop/CSCE 704/Model/704papers/2112.02125v3.pdf>), 18 pages; Pearce et al.

**Evidence.** The framework supplies vulnerable code, defect information, and varying prompt templates to multiple LLMs, then checks compilation, security, and functionality separately (pp. 3–8). Synthetic and hand-written tasks are substantially easier than real project repairs, and context selection becomes a limitation when whole files do not fit (pp. 9–12). A reported 100% scenario-level repair result means at least one candidate among many passed the selected tests, not that every generated patch was correct. The paper explicitly identifies implausible patches that passed regression tests, including changes that remove useful behavior or introduce untested bugs (pp. 10–12). It cautions that fixing the original crashing input is not proof that the vulnerability is absent (p. 12). Appendices provide detailed result tables and example repairs (pp. 15–18).

**Applicability and decision.** Relevant to building and repairing the service, not to the classifier's features or threshold. A timeout fix that skips classification or returns a constant label may pass a superficial health test while failing the challenge. Verify end-to-end response semantics and behavior on malformed, oversized, and representative valid inputs after changes. Do not count a passing unit test, one favorable sample, or a syntactically valid HTTP response as evidence of TPR/FPR compliance. No runtime LLM is justified by this paper.

## 7. Pop Quiz! Can a Large Language Model Help With Reverse Engineering?

**Source:** [2202.01142v1.pdf](</Users/seshadithyasaravanan/Desktop/CSCE 704/Model/704papers/2202.01142v1.pdf>), 18 pages; Pearce et al.

**Evidence.** The study asks an LLM to infer program purposes, capabilities, and values from source and decompiled code. Early open-ended demonstrations motivate a more systematic quiz with both positive and negative questions and program randomization (pp. 3–8). It evaluates security-related programs, industrial-control examples, and real malware capabilities, observing failures on negated questions and sensitivity to representation (pp. 8–11). It reports 72,754 correct responses out of 136,260 questions, or 53.39%; these are mixed reverse-engineering questions rather than malware verdicts (p. 11). Limitations and an embedding comparison follow (pp. 11–13), with further example programs and per-question heatmaps in the appendix (pp. 16–18).

**Applicability and decision.** This does not support putting LLM decompilation/explanation into a 1 GB, five-second malware-classification service. The question accuracy is not TPR or specificity. LLMs can assist offline analyst interpretation, with independent verification, but inference should use bounded local features and a validated statistical model. Decompilation and capability-extraction systems require their own measured budget and incremental-detection evidence before adoption.

## 8. Lost at C: A User Study on the Security Implications of Large Language Model Code Assistants

**Source:** [2208.09727v4.pdf](</Users/seshadithyasaravanan/Desktop/CSCE 704/Model/704papers/2208.09727v4.pdf>), 18 pages; Sandoval et al.

**Evidence.** The experiment compares 29 assisted and 29 control participants implementing a C shopping-list data structure, plus fully generated comparison solutions (pp. 4–8). It separates implementation/compilation, basic versus expanded functional tests, security weaknesses, and bug density (pp. 8–11). Manual review and instrumented analysis find vulnerabilities that ordinary functionality tests miss (pp. 9–12). In this narrow setting, severe-bug incidence in the assisted group stays within the paper's 10% non-inferiority margin. The authors trace bugs from both human and model contributions and discuss task design, student participants, and incomplete interaction capture as limits (pp. 12–13). The appendix covers ethics, weakness taxonomy, completion time, and per-function results (pp. 16–18).

**Applicability and decision.** This concerns development assistance, not malware-detection models. It neither proves generated code is secure nor contradicts the need for concrete code review. Use assisted coding with tests for memory/resource boundaries and public API behavior. The service's actual properties determine readiness; whether a line was written by a human or an LLM does not determine its correctness. There is no PE model or challenge-compatible benchmark to integrate.

## 9. TROJAN PUZZLE: Covertly Poisoning Code-Suggestion Models

**Source:** [2301.02344v2.pdf](</Users/seshadithyasaravanan/Desktop/CSCE 704/Model/704papers/2301.02344v2.pdf>), 19 pages; Aghakhani et al.

**Evidence.** The threat model is malicious content entering a code model's fine-tuning corpus. COVERT places payload examples in docstrings; TROJAN PUZZLE uses learned substitution patterns so complete targeted insecure strings need not appear in the poison corpus (pp. 2–6). Four CWE trials vary model size, fine-tuning data volume, epochs, and target specificity (pp. 6–11). Attack@k measures whether at least one of k generated completions contains a target payload; it is not a classifier false-negative rate. Dataset filtering and fine-pruning defenses have limitations, including assumptions that cleaning data are representative and clean (pp. 11–13). Appendices provide per-trial plots, pruning details, and a program-committee meta-review (pp. 15–19).

**Applicability and decision.** Indirect supply-chain relevance: preserve training provenance, avoid self-labeling arbitrary submitted samples into a new model, verify model artifacts, and review copied code and rules. Its attacks and reported success rates are specific to code generation; they do not demonstrate evasion against LightGBM or establish a PE-defense benefit from fine-pruning. Do not add a code-generation model or this poisoning framework to production inference.

## 10. AUTOATTACKER: A Large Language Model Guided System to Implement Automatic Cyber-attacks

**Source:** [2403.01038v1.pdf](</Users/seshadithyasaravanan/Desktop/CSCE 704/Model/704papers/2403.01038v1.pdf>), 19 pages; Xu et al.

**Evidence.** This is a multi-step attack-automation system with planning, summarization, command generation, execution feedback, and experience retrieval (pp. 5–8). Experiments use prepared enterprise-network tasks, repeat each task three times, and allow up to 30 interactions (pp. 8–10). Results and ablations analyze task success, interactions, and API costs, not PE-detection quality (pp. 10–12). The limitations explicitly say the victim environment was configured insecurely, including disabling antivirus; results therefore do not establish bypass of a well-protected environment (p. 12). The rest covers implications, references, and example task specifications and interactions (pp. 13–19).

**Applicability and decision.** Out of scope for a standalone static PE classifier. It supports the general need to model adaptive adversaries, but contributes neither a PE feature extractor nor a compliant detector. Its remote LLM dependence, multiple interactions, and environment execution are unsuitable for the required self-contained request path. Do not confuse successful post-compromise automation with evasion of this detector, and do not integrate attack execution into the image.

## 11. Shallow Security: on the Creation of Adversarial Variants to Evade Machine Learning-Based Malware Detectors

**Source:** [3375894.3375898.pdf](</Users/seshadithyasaravanan/Desktop/CSCE 704/Model/704papers/3375894.3375898.pdf>), 9 pages; Ceschin et al.

**Evidence.** The study attacks the 2019 MLSEC MalConv, non-negative MalConv, and EMBER LightGBM models using 50 malware samples, with behavioral validation (pp. 1–3). Simple appended bytes or benign strings affect decisions, while mutable metadata and wrapping malware as a resource expose weaknesses in an outer-file-only representation (pp. 3–5). The paper reports 150 successful model/sample combinations after wrapping and adding benign content; this is a small, targeted historical attack benchmark, not an estimate for all malware. On 448 Windows EXEs and 2,422 DLLs, the two neural models show very high FPR, while the evaluated LightGBM has 0% FPR on that narrow collection (p. 3). Packing experiments reveal reliance on packer cues and illustrate that suspicious packaging is not equivalent to maliciousness (p. 4). The recommendations include malicious-feature presence, robust representations, embedded-resource inspection, and variant-resilience evaluation (pp. 6–7). The final appendix lists per-sample verdicts/confidences (p. 9).

**Applicability and decision.** Directly relevant. Evaluate benign-content dilution, overlays, header changes, resource-embedded PEs, and known benign packed files. A bounded embedded-PE pass can address an identified blind spot, but the existence of a nested executable is not by itself a malware verdict: installers and self-extracting archives can contain legitimate PEs. Do not assume a raw-byte neural model or non-negative weights guarantee robustness. Any extra verdict path must be calibrated as part of the combined detector, including its FPR and processing cost.

## 12. No Need to Teach New Tricks to Old Malware: Winning an Evasion Challenge with XOR-based Adversarial Samples

**Source:** [3433667.3433669.pdf](</Users/seshadithyasaravanan/Desktop/CSCE 704/Model/704papers/3433667.3433669.pdf>), 10 pages; Ceschin et al.

**Evidence.** The 2020 challenge accepted detectors with FPR < 1%, FNR < 10%, and classification within five seconds; this recall requirement was weaker than the current user's TPR ≥ 95% (p. 2). The paper's NeedForSpeed model uses selected EMBER metadata and import-derived textual features with a 100-tree random forest; regional training/evaluation differences materially affect performance (pp. 3–4). Some pefile parses exceeded five seconds, while the corresponding LIEF parses were approximately two seconds, and parser representation differences required feature changes (p. 4). Its model detected 88.91% of 594 prior-year adversarial samples, yet was later evaded by the authors' newly constructed samples (p. 5). Outer-PE camouflage, import dilution, and simple payload encodings defeated complementary feature views (pp. 5–7). Retraining on prior adversarial files reduced attack FNR but increased FPR on the benign set from 0.1% to 78.54% in their experiment (p. 8). The paper recommends payload carving, efficient decoding, and less mutable features while emphasizing time constraints (p. 9).

**Applicability and decision.** Directly relevant to both architecture and deployment. Pin the parser and feature schema to the training artifact and measure malformed-file costs. Preserve enough request budget for a bounded resource/embedded-PE inspection; any decoding must have explicit byte, candidate, and recursion caps. Detect actual payload content where possible instead of equating a resource, entropy value, or API import with malware. Include benign mimics and installers in validation. Do not deploy adversarial retraining or an OR-combined heuristic layer solely because it catches attack samples: its aggregate benign FPR must remain within budget. The paper's threshold description is also not a portable value for a different model.

## 13. Moving Target Defense Against Adversarial Machine Learning

**Source:** [3474370.3485662.pdf](</Users/seshadithyasaravanan/Desktop/CSCE 704/Model/704papers/3474370.3485662.pdf>), 2 pages; Chhabra and Mohapatra.

**Evidence.** The paper models defender selection among multiple learners and attacker selection among attacks using a transferability/accuracy matrix, then learns a selection policy with PPO (pp. 1–2). The preliminary experiment constructs a 50-option matrix, deliberately gives some defenses minimum accuracy near 0.8 while most attacks reduce accuracy to about 0.3, and shows the agent learns the favorable choices (p. 2). It is a simulated policy-selection demonstration, not measured malware robustness; realistic switching cost and unknown attack distributions are not validated here.

**Applicability and decision.** Do not use random switching or online RL as a substitute for a calibrated detector. Multiple resident models increase memory pressure, switching may increase latency, and stochastic verdicts complicate reproducible FPR/TPR evaluation. A small ensemble is worth testing only if its members have empirically complementary errors on held-out PE and adversarial data, with a jointly calibrated decision rule and measured resource use. This paper does not establish such complementarity or challenge compliance.

## Synthesis for implementation

1. **Start with a reproducible static PE baseline.** The strongest directly applicable evidence favors measuring a structured-feature tree model before adding a raw-byte neural model, LLM, dynamic sandbox, or expensive disassembly. This is a candidate-selection rationale, not a claim that all tree models meet the target.
2. **Treat parsing as an adversarial input boundary.** Use the exact feature schema and compatible parser version; cap bytes, sections, directories, imports, strings, nesting, candidates, and total work. A nominally fast predictor can still time out during feature extraction.
3. **Evaluate multiple file views cautiously.** Raw whole-file features, a validated PE-extent view, and bounded embedded-payload views may expose complementary signals. Removing an overlay or normalizing headers can also remove real evidence or change the training distribution; promote a view only after testing its incremental benefit and aggregate FPR. Bounded inspection is evidence-informed hardening, not certified robustness.
4. **Keep false-positive control central.** Do not automatically classify all packed, high-entropy, malformed, or resource-bearing files as malicious. Do not assume OR-ing individually useful signals preserves the 1% FPR target. Calibrate the final pipeline, then freeze it before the final held-out evaluation.
5. **Separate operational validation from statistical validation.** A successful Docker health check proves only service operation. Use representative labeled PE files for TPR/FPR and adversarial variants for robustness; include failures and timeouts in reported outcomes. Benchmark the final container under its actual RAM/CPU settings, including feature extraction, queueing assumptions, and response serialization.
6. **Do not claim the target from papers.** Historical challenge eligibility, family-signature quality, LLM repair success, and simulated RL accuracy are different measurements. The product can be implementation-complete while the quality target remains unverified when representative labeled evaluation data are unavailable.
